Minimum spanning trees, step by step

Connect every vertex of a weighted, undirected graph using edges of the smallest possible total weight. A spanning tree of n vertices always has n − 1 edges and no cycle. All four algorithms rest on two facts: the lightest edge crossing a cut is safe to add (cut property), and the heaviest edge on a cycle is safe to leave out (cycle property).

    Cut property

    Split the vertices into two groups (a cut). The lightest edge with one end on each side belongs to some MST. Kruskal, Prim and Borůvka all add only such edges, which is why each edge they add is safe.

    Cycle property

    On any cycle, the heaviest edge (if it is strictly heaviest) belongs to no MST: swapping it for another edge of the cycle gives a lighter tree. Kruskal rejects exactly such edges, and reverse-delete deletes them.

    Is the MST unique?

    If all edge weights are different, there is exactly one MST. With ties there may be several, all with the same total weight. Try Equal weights: the triangle a, b, c has three edges of weight 1, and any two of them form an MST.

    MST vs shortest paths

    An MST minimises the total weight of the network, not the distance between two vertices. In the textbook graph, Kruskal’s tree joins a to e by a–h–g–f–c–d–e (weight 31), while the shortest path a–h–g–f–e weighs 21.
